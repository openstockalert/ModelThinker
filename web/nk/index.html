Ch. 28 · Learning & search

When smart hill-climbing gets stuck

You're designing a product with 6 yes/no decisions.
The rules keep changing under your feet. Try to reach the best version.

Six decisions: 🎨 colour · 📦 size · ⚙️ features · 🎵 sound · 🚀 speed · 💰 price. Every combination has a "fitness" (call it customer love, or evolutionary fitness — same math). If each decision were independent, you could just tweak them one at a time and reach the best version. But when decisions interact — a loud sound is great on a fast big product but obnoxious on a small quiet one — you land in a maze of local peaks. Try climbing to the top:

Turn K up gradually. At K = 0, every card shows one obvious best flip (green +something). You'll click your way to the top in ~6 moves. At K = 3, most cards show red or zero deltas and you get stuck on a mediocre peak with no way up. That's the whole story: interactions kill greedy search. Try the same K a few times — different random starts strand you on different local peaks. That's why diverse teams beat single searchers on rugged problems (Page, The Difference).

Race three strategies on the same terrain

Now give three searchers the same budget of moves on the same landscape:

  • Hill climb — always take the best neighbouring bit-flip. Greedy. Stops at the first local peak it finds.
  • Random restart — hill climb, but from ten different random starts. Diversity insurance.
  • Long jumps — mostly hill climb, occasionally flip many bits at once. Costly, but escapes local peaks.

The management-strategy translation. Kauffman & Levinthal's argument, restated: on a smooth landscape (few interactions, low K), incremental optimisation wins. On a rugged landscape (product features that constrain each other, policies that ripple), pure incrementalism strands you on a local hilltop. That's why radically-different competitors sometimes outperform "best-in-class" incumbents — they climb a different hill entirely.

Diverse teams > homogeneous experts (on rugged terrain)

Page's The Difference generalises this: if smart searchers all start from the same place with the same heuristic, they all get stuck on the same local peak. Different searchers get stuck on different peaks — and can share their moves. The mathematical case for diversity, hiding in an NK landscape.
