The polygon paradox

People are pretty tolerant. Neighbourhoods are pretty segregated.
How can both be true?

Let's play a game. Below is a little neighbourhood of blue and orange faces. Each face peeks at its eight closest neighbours and has one tiny preference: "I'd like at least some of my neighbours to be like me. Doesn't have to be most. Just, y'know, some."

A face that's happy 🙂 stays put. One that isn't 🙁 hops to a random empty spot. That's the entire rule. Now watch what happens.

Try this. Set the slider to 30%. That's not "I want to be in the majority" — it's "I'm fine being 70% minority, as long as I'm not almost completely alone." Hit play and give it a few thousand moves. The segregation meter should climb to ~0.80. Nobody in this simulation asked for that. Yet here we are.

What just happened?

The trick is that "unhappy" doesn't require racism — it just requires a lower bound. Some blues will land next to too many oranges by chance. They move. Now the spot they vacated is empty; a nearby orange whose neighbourhood was fine is now surrounded by blanks — its ratio changed too. It might move. Small local moves ripple. Clusters form and, once they form, they self-reinforce because clusters are the very thing that makes agents happy.

Thomas Schelling (1971) gave us the sharp version of this: micro-motives don't equal macro-behaviour. Individually reasonable preferences aggregate into outcomes nobody wants. That's why "just being tolerant" doesn't dissolve segregation — the geometry itself is against you.

"The extreme cases are easier to understand than the mild ones… you can be color-blind and still end up in a segregated neighbourhood, because your neighbours aren't."
— Thomas Schelling, Micromotives and Macrobehavior (1978)

Slide the dial slowly

Here's the surprise. As you crank up the tolerance dial, the equilibrium segregation doesn't rise linearly. It jumps. Below ~10% almost nothing happens. Above ~50% the model can't even settle — too many shapes are permanently frustrated. In the middle band, the same rule that felt harmless produces dramatic separation.

Worth pausing on. The Schelling model is a caricature — real housing choice involves prices, schools, family, commute. It isn't a literal account of segregation. What it does tell you is that "the outcome must reflect what people wanted" is often just wrong. Institutions (school zoning, mortgage lending, tax incentives) shape the moves; nobody's individual preference graph explains the map.

Reader's homework

  1. Set τ to 90% in the sandbox. The system can never settle — shapes are almost always unhappy. Does the meter still rise?
  2. Set density to 98% and τ to 40%. Watch how "trapped" shapes behave. This is the housing-market friction argument.
  3. Try tiny grids (size 15) vs bigger (size 60). Small samples get louder-looking segregation by chance — a CLT story hiding in the corner.
