Ch. 18 · Systems dynamics

Loops, delays, and policies that backfire

Three simple ideas — stocks, flows, feedback loops — plus one modifier: delay.
Together they explain why so much of the world refuses to behave the way your intuition insists it should.

Jay Forrester invented this toolkit at MIT in the late 1950s and used it to explain why cities decline, why supply chains explode, why forests burn bigger when you suppress small fires. Below are three facts the toolkit makes obvious — and that most people get wrong on first look.

Fact 1: A bathtub keeps filling even while you turn the faucet down

A stock is anything that accumulates — water in a tub, CO₂ in the atmosphere, people in a city, cash in the bank, debt. A flow is a rate — litres per minute, tonnes per year, hires per month. The rule is laughably simple: the stock grows whenever inflow is bigger than outflow, period. Yet John Sterman found that even MIT engineering students, shown an identical bathtub with the labels swapped to "atmospheric CO₂" and "emissions," confidently concluded that falling emissions means falling concentrations.

It doesn't. Below: a tub with 1000 litres, draining at 40/year. The faucet starts at 100/year and ticks down as you crank the handle. Pick an "aggressiveness" and watch what the stock actually does.

Try "Gentle cut". You cut emissions by 2 units every single year — a steady, visible effort. The stock still climbs for about 30 years before finally turning around. Even "Aggressive" takes 7 years to peak. Only "Match the drain" (instantly dropping inflow to equal outflow) stabilises the stock immediately. The lesson for climate, debt, city population, inventory: stabilising a stock means getting the flow all the way down to the outflow rate, not merely reducing it.

Fact 2: Delays turn gentle correction into violent oscillation

Everyone has lived this one. Shower is cold. You turn the knob toward hot. Nothing happens, so you turn it further. Scalding water hits. You panic, yank it back, freezing water hits. Repeat until you give up or shower partially dressed. The feedback was correct in sign and useless in timing.

This is exactly why central banks fight inflation badly when the lag between rate changes and effect is long, why boom-bust real-estate cycles exist, and why adding staff to a late software project makes it later.

Fact 3: Spray the pest, get MORE pests

Here's the one that makes the whole chapter worth it. Rabbits (🐰) are a crop pest. Foxes (🦊) are their natural predator. The populations oscillate — classic predator/prey. A farmer decides to spray a broad-spectrum poison that kills rabbits AND foxes equally. Average pest population goes up. Not short-term: average, over many cycles.

This is Vito Volterra's result (1926) and it's the structural reason the DDT era produced record spider-mite outbreaks, and why integrated pest management replaced broad-spectrum spraying. Below: slide the spray dial and watch the long-run average rabbit count rise.

The lesson, in one line: if your intervention hits a problem and its natural check with similar force, you may be strengthening the problem. Pests breed fast, predators breed slow — the regulator recovers slower than what it was regulating. Same shape applies to broad-spectrum antibiotics (clearing competing flora, enabling C. difficile), suppressing every small forest fire (fuel accumulates, next one is catastrophic), and across-the-board budget cuts that remove the monitoring function along with the waste.

The real power: pattern recognition

Systems dynamics' quiet gift is a short list of loop patterns that recur everywhere. Once you've seen them you recognise them instantly:

  • Limits to growth — reinforcing loop meets balancing loop. Every startup eventually hits it.
  • Fixes that fail — a fix relieves the symptom but worsens the cause with a delay. Borrowing to cover a shortfall.
  • Shifting the burden — external fix erodes internal capacity. Consultants replace in-house skill. Painkillers mask the pain and delay the diagnosis.
  • Drifting goals — the standard adjusts toward whatever you're actually achieving. How safety cultures decay.
  • Escalation — each side responds to the gap with the other. Arms races, price wars.

Donella Meadows ranked policy leverage points weakest to strongest: parameters (tax rates) → buffer sizes → delays → loop strengths → new loops → rules → goals → the paradigm. Note that shortening a delay sits above every parameter tweak — exactly what Fact 2 just showed you.
